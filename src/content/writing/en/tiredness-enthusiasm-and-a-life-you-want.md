In the video I move quickly through things a person might look forward to: push-ups in the morning, a book, making a video, writing an article, building a company, spending time with children, a partner or friends.

The thread connecting them is enthusiasm. The feeling that you have a part in making the life you are waking up to.

I describe that feeling in spiritual language, as a fire inside. The article keeps that personal meaning. But the opening claim that the most tired people are those who are not creating their own lives needs a limit: **fatigue is not evidence of a failed mindset.**

## The body is not a motivation score

The NHS lists many possible contributors to tiredness, including insufficient sleep, stress, illness, hormonal changes and medicines. Persistent unexplained fatigue, particularly when it affects daily life, deserves medical attention rather than self-diagnosis. [1]

A person can love their family, care about their work and still be exhausted. Someone in treatment, a parent short on sleep or a person with an ongoing condition does not need another reason to feel inadequate.

The contrast in my video makes sense as a reflection on engagement. It cannot diagnose why someone has no energy, and enthusiasm is not a substitute for treatment or rest.

## What are you looking forward to?

With that distinction in place, the useful question is smaller than a complete life reinvention: is there something in the week that you actually want to meet?

It might be one page of writing. It might be dinner with someone you miss. It may have nothing to do with productivity at all. Spending time with my family appears in the same list as building a business because making a life is wider than producing work.

For someone whose days are full of obligations, choosing one thing can be a way of noticing their own preferences again. That is an invitation to reflect, not a promise that the body will suddenly supply more energy.

## Keep the experiment kind

Choose something that fits the energy and resources you have. Afterwards, ask whether it felt worthwhile. There is no need to score your character from the result. If today calls for rest, resting does not cancel your ambition or your love for the people around you.

A small plan can also reveal a practical obstacle: no quiet time, too many commitments, a task that was never yours to carry. Those deserve practical answers, not just a stronger motivational speech.

I make a related distinction in [trying, doing and visible progress](/writing/trying-doing-visible-progress/). Here I would add one more boundary: a meaningful action is still meaningful when it is small, private or followed by a long rest.

The spark is an invitation. It should never become an accusation.
