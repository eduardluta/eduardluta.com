During the period when I was accompanying my mother to oncology, I met relatives who said they were not telling a parent what illness they had. They thought they were protecting them.

That is what I talk about in this video. It is a personal account of conversations I encountered, not a survey of families or hospitals.

I understand the fear behind it. You love somebody. You do not want to be the person who brings frightening news into the room. But I kept coming back to the same question: whose life is being discussed?

## The person in the bed still has a voice

In the clip I use a sporting image. The rest of us may be supporters, advisers or coaches. The patient is the person actually living it.

The image has a limit. **Cancer outcomes are not a test of how hard somebody wants to live.** A person who becomes sicker has not failed to fight. The point I want to preserve is dignity and participation, not a burden to cure yourself through determination.

The World Medical Association’s Declaration of Lisbon supports a capable adult’s participation in decisions and access to understandable information. It also recognises an explicit wish not to receive information and the patient’s choice of who may receive it on their behalf.[1]

So “tell the truth” needs a second question: how does this person want the conversation to happen? Some want detail. Others want the main points first, or a trusted person beside them. Their preference should not be replaced by our guess about what they can handle.

## Do not make a frightening conversation a lonely one

If your family is struggling with this, ask the treating team to help plan the discussion. Explain your worries privately, then make room for the patient’s wishes. A clinician can explain the diagnosis, uncertainty and options; relatives can offer presence, practical help and the knowledge of what matters to that person.

The National Cancer Institute’s patient guidance encourages people to tell their team how much information they want, prepare questions and ask for information they can revisit later. It also describes how patients and families sometimes hide distress to protect one another.[2]

A practical starting point could be: “Would you like the doctor to explain the details now? Who would you like with you?” These are example questions, not words from my family’s conversations. They invite a preference instead of assuming one.

Allow pauses. Ask what was understood. Arrange a next conversation when needed. Truth does not require delivering every detail in one overwhelming speech, and compassion does not require inventing a different diagnosis.

## More than a treatment decision

In the video I also talk about the space honesty can create for family conversations, unfinished matters and wishes about the future. That does not mean assuming every diagnosis is terminal. It means allowing the person to decide what they want to discuss, while their own voice is present.

Privacy remains theirs too. Knowing a relative’s diagnosis does not make it ours to announce to everybody else. When decision-making capacity is impaired or an emergency changes the situation, involve the clinical team and the applicable consent process rather than trying to settle it through a general video.

My [reflection on emotions during illness](/writing/anger-during-illness-support-without-pressure/) comes back to this: support should make room for the person, including feelings that are difficult for us to hear.

Love can sit beside somebody while the truth is spoken. It does not have to speak over them.
