A king finally has a son. People arrive with gifts. A witch offers what sounds like the greatest blessing: whatever the boy touches, he will become the best at it.

In the story I tell in the video, that gift turns into a curse. Piano, football, painting, singing. The prince keeps moving. There is always something else that catches his eye.

I am retelling a tale here, not reporting a historical event. I have not established its original author. What stayed with me was the uncomfortable question inside it: what if your ability to begin keeps helping you escape the difficulty of continuing?

## Starting and staying are different skills

Curiosity is useful. Trying things can help you find what you enjoy. Skills can also combine: learning a little about writing, design and technology may help you make something you could not make with just one of them.

But exploring is different from changing direction whenever progress stops feeling easy. If every project ends at its first difficult part, the next exciting idea can become a very elegant excuse.

The question is not whether you should choose one activity for the rest of your life. It is whether you can stay long enough to learn what the next difficult part actually requires.

## Twenty hours is a starting framework, not a certificate

In the clip I contrast learning the basics in 20 hours with developing mastery over 10,000 hours. Those numbers need care.

Josh Kaufman’s [*The First 20 Hours*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/312040/the-first-20-hours-by-josh-kaufman/) proposes breaking a skill into parts and practising the useful basics. That is the book’s approach. A publisher’s description is not evidence that every learner can reach every chosen standard in exactly 20 hours.

Likewise, **10,000 hours is not a universal threshold that automatically turns you into a master**. A meta-analysis by Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald found that deliberate practice matters, but does not by itself explain all differences in performance. Its authors later corrected parts of their statistical analysis without changing that overall conclusion. The research does not offer an hours-to-fame conversion table.

Do the calendar calculation anyway, because it makes the commitment concrete. In a hypothetical schedule of **five one-hour sessions each week**, 20 hours takes **four weeks**. At that same rate, 10,000 hours takes **2,000 weeks**, about **38.5 years using 52 weeks per year**. These are time totals, not two scientifically established levels of ability.

## Choose the next standard you can actually check

Instead of declaring “I will master the guitar,” choose a smaller performance you can test: play a selected piece through at a chosen tempo, for example. Record an attempt, identify the difficult part, practise it and compare another attempt. The target and schedule should fit the actual skill and your starting point.

Then make a decision with evidence. Continue because the work still matters. Change the method because it is not helping. Or stop because you have learned enough to know this is not your direction. Stopping for a considered reason is different from fleeing every uncomfortable beginning.

My [five-year video](/writing/one-hour-a-day-five-years-the-real-math/) asks for commitment. Here, the prince asks what that commitment is for.

Being able to start everything does not decide what deserves your next hour.
