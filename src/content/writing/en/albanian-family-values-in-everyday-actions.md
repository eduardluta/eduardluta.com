In this video I respond to a discussion about the definition of an Albanian family. My starting point is the husband-and-wife model I describe in the clip. Then I ask much more of it than a label.

Faithfulness. Love. Respect. Honest work. Parents who spend time with their children. People who care about their community and the country they want to leave behind.

That is the part of the argument I want to develop here. A definition can be easy to repeat. Daily conduct is harder to hide behind.

## A value should describe something we do

If I say fidelity matters, it has to mean something when nobody is applauding. If I say respect matters, it has to appear in how I speak during a disagreement. If I say children come first, there should be room for them in the day, not only in a photograph.

In the clip I also talk about faith and caring for our bodies. These are parts of the values I am describing, not a scientific checklist for ranking families. Appearance, income or a difficult period of illness cannot establish somebody’s worth as a parent.

The useful standard is conduct we can actually examine: do we keep agreements, share responsibility, speak honestly and make room for the people who depend on us?

## What does “being there for the children” look like?

Presence is more than occupying the same room. A small example would be putting a conversation with your child ahead of scrolling for a moment, listening to the answer and asking a question about what they actually said. That is an illustration, not a scene claimed from my home.

UNICEF’s guidance for communicating with young children recommends attentive listening, age-appropriate language and comments about behaviour rather than attacks on the child as a person. It also emphasises the example adults set through their actions.[1]

There is a practical difference between “You are impossible” and describing the specific thing that needs to change. The second leaves room to explain, listen and repair. A child should not have to earn a basic sense of being respected by making the adults look good.

## Pride without pretending the home is perfect

The video is forceful about raising standards. That should begin with our own behaviour. It is much easier to announce what other people ought to be than to notice where our own promises have become empty.

A family can have disagreements. The question is whether people can address them without humiliation or fear. A hard week does not cancel a loving relationship; a proud label does not excuse repeated mistreatment.

Nor does this essay provide a legal definition of family or a verdict on every household. Families live in different arrangements and circumstances. The responsibilities I am discussing—care, honesty, respect and attention to children—deserve to be taken seriously wherever children and adults depend on one another.

My [relationship workshop reflection](/writing/relationship-workshop-planning-a-life-together/) asks a related question: have we actually discussed the life we say we want to share?

I want pride in family to ask something of me. Less performance on behalf of a word. More evidence of its meaning in the way we live together.
