A piece of bad news arrives and suddenly everything seems to belong to it. The work in front of you. The conversation with your family. The whole future.

In the video, I start by promising three things to do and end up describing four. The extra step matters: after finding some steadiness, put the options on paper. The point is to give yourself enough room to decide what happens next.

## First, check whether action is needed now

A pause makes sense only when waiting is safe. An immediate danger, a medical emergency or an urgent safeguarding concern needs appropriate help now. A breathing exercise cannot replace that response.

For a problem that does allow a pause, I mention walking, breathing, sitting quietly and getting some distance. These are ways of trying to settle enough to think. They do not require you to pretend the news is small or stop feeling upset.

The World Health Organization's stress guide includes grounding, noticing difficult thoughts and acting on what matters to you. That supports the modest idea of practising useful coping skills; it does not promise that a short exercise will solve the event itself.

## Step away without abandoning the problem

There is a difference between leaving a problem for a little while and refusing to face it. If no immediate decision is required, a walk or a break can create a boundary around the first reaction.

Make the return concrete: after the break, I will write down what I know and identify the next conversation. Otherwise, a pause can become an indefinite postponement.

## Keep one crisis from taking over every role

In the video I talk about being a father, a partner, a sibling and a person with work and health to look after. A serious problem in one part of life can affect all of those roles. It does not erase them.

This is not a demand to perform normally through distress. It is a reason to ask for practical help. Someone might collect the children, cover a meeting or bring a meal while you handle the urgent part. Protecting the ordinary can include letting another person carry some of it.

## Write options, then choose one next step

My last suggestion is simple: get a sheet of paper. Use three columns—what is known, what needs checking, and what can be done next. Keeping these separate stops a frightening possibility from quietly becoming an established fact.

For example, if a business customer cancels a project, the fact is the cancelled work. “The whole business is finished” is a conclusion to examine. Next steps might include checking the cash position, speaking to the customer and contacting other leads. This is an illustrative example, not an event reported in the video.

You do not need a complete solution before doing the first useful thing. You may need a trusted person or professional beside you. If distress is persistent or making daily life difficult, seek qualified support. Asking for that help belongs inside the plan.

The same distinction appears in my note on [visible progress](/writing/trying-doing-visible-progress/): name the action you can take, without pretending you control the entire outcome.
