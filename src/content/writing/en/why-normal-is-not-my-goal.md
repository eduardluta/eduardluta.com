Why do you always want to do more, or do it differently? Why can't you be normal?

That question is the spark for this video. I look at habits people can accept without much examination—how we spend time, how we move, what we consume—and ask why fitting in should be the highest ambition.

But a challenge is stronger when the numbers beneath it are honest. The clip includes several sweeping statistics about an average person's sugar, weight, steps, work and screen time. It does not establish a consistent population, period or source for that bundle. I am not presenting those figures here as verified facts about a “normal person.”

## Common is not the same as desirable

A habit can be familiar without serving you well. It can also look unimpressive from outside while fitting a life full of responsibilities you cannot see. The point is to examine a habit, not turn somebody else's body, job or circumstances into a moral verdict.

For me, the more useful question is: if I deliberately chose this week, would it look like the week I am repeating?

That brings the argument back to choices I can actually observe.

## Use a real reference where a real one exists

For physical activity, WHO recommends adults aim for 150–300 minutes of moderate aerobic activity weekly, or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity, or an equivalent combination. It also recommends muscle-strengthening work on at least two days and emphasises that some activity is better than none. These are general guidelines, not a verdict on an individual's health or a complete personal programme.

For free sugars, WHO recommends less than 10% of total energy intake, with a further reduction below 5% offering additional benefits. Free sugars include added sugars and those in honey, syrups and fruit juices; the term is not the same as every sugar naturally present in all foods.

Those recommendations answer defined questions. They do not validate the video's unrelated claims about what an average person does across an entire lifetime.

## A week gives you a smaller, clearer calculation

Suppose, purely as an illustration, I move 20 minutes a day from passive scrolling into something I have chosen. **20 × 7 = 140 minutes per week**, or **2 hours and 20 minutes**. That time could be reading, calling someone, making something or moving in a way that suits me.

It is not a claim that I measured your screen time. It is not a health threshold either: 140 minutes of unspecified activity should not be confused with meeting the aerobic guideline above. It simply makes a small choice visible on a weekly scale.

## Difference needs a direction

Being unusual is not an achievement by itself. A habit deserves keeping because of what it contributes, not because it makes me look different. Reading something carefully, looking after relationships and building useful work give the word “different” a direction.

I want to choose more of my life deliberately. That is the challenge I would keep from the video—even after removing the impressive numbers that have not earned our trust.

It connects to [why I follow people online](/writing/why-i-follow-people-online/): attention is something I can decide to give.
