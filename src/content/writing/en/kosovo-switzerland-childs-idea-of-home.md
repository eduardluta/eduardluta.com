In the car, I ask Marin whether he would rather live in Kosovo or Switzerland. He chooses Kosovo. The exchange lasts only seconds. My caption says that sometimes things are very simple.

I do not want to turn his answer into a national ranking. One child's preference is one child's preference, at one moment. It does make me think about what adults leave out when we compare places.

We reach for salaries, infrastructure, schools, prices. Those things matter. But “where is life better?” also contains another question: where do I feel part of life?

## Two comparisons can both matter

An adult may be thinking about a contract, a rent payment or the reliability of a service. A child may be thinking about who will be there after school and where they know how to join in.

That is my reflection on the exchange, not a claim that I can read every reason behind Marin's answer. The useful response is to ask more, not decide that thirteen seconds have settled where a family should live.

I have done an actual financial comparison in [the Kosovo–Germany grocery article](/writing/kosovo-germany-grocery-prices/). It shows why prices need to be considered alongside income. This video asks for a different layer. We cannot calculate belonging by dividing a salary by a shopping basket.

## Relationships belong in the decision

Harvard's Center on the Developing Child describes supportive relationships and active skill-building as foundations of resilience. That supports paying attention to a child's dependable connections. It does not prove that one country is better for children, or that moving country necessarily harms them. [Working Paper 13](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/resources/working-paper/supportive-relationships-and-active-skill-building-strengthen-the-foundations-of-resilience/).

For a family considering a move, I would translate that into specific questions. Who can the child turn to? Which familiar relationships will continue? What will the first ordinary week look like, once the excitement of arriving is over?

Suppose a family has better financial prospects in one place and more nearby relatives in another. Neither advantage cancels the other. Write down the actual arrangements: childcare, travel time, calls, visits, school transition and opportunities to make friends. “We will stay connected” becomes more useful when someone knows how it will happen.

This is an illustrative planning exercise. It is not a description of a move our family has decided to make.

## Listen without making the child carry the decision

Asking for a preference does not mean handing a child responsibility for the family's future. Adults still have to consider safety, income, education, housing and the circumstances they cannot choose away.

But listening should change something. If a child names a person or a place they would miss, that is a concrete concern we can take seriously. Explain what is decided, what is still open, and what can be kept familiar.

That balance is connected to [responsibility with support](/writing/teaching-children-responsibility-with-support/): a child gets a voice without having to carry an adult's burden.

The answer in the car is short. The attention it deserves is longer.
