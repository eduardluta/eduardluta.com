“Kejt mirë, pak shtremt.” Everything is fine, a little crooked.

That is the caption on this eight-second video of a bust of Jusuf Gërvalla. The name on the pedestal matters. This is a place intended to honour someone, and my passing comment notices a detail in how the place appears.

The joke is small. The question behind it is whether we give the same attention to looking after a monument as we give to putting it there.

## A name before a piece of street furniture

Jusuf Gërvalla was a writer, poet and journalist as well as a political activist. The Kosovo Prime Minister's Office remembered those parts of his life at its 17 January 2026 commemoration of the killing of Jusuf and Bardhosh Gërvalla in 1982.[1]

That short context changes how I want to look at the bust. It represents a life and work, not simply an object to arrange in a square. A name on a pedestal can be the beginning of learning about someone, rather than the end of remembering them.

## What this little video can actually show

The frame shows the bust, its stone pedestal, the paved setting and trees around it. It is a brief view, not a survey. Camera position and perspective affect how straight objects appear; I have no level measurement or inspection report here.

So the caption should stay a wry observation. It is not proof that the structure is unsafe, evidence of a contractor's mistake, or a finding that someone misused money. Those would be much larger claims requiring different evidence.

## Care should survive the ceremony

The useful next questions are ordinary ones. Is the memorial in good condition? Is its inscription readable? Does anything need cleaning, adjustment or professional attention? Who looks after it, and when was it last checked?

Those are things to establish on site, not defects I am claiming to have demonstrated in these eight seconds. Asking them is one way to take the person being commemorated seriously.

I come back to the same thought in [the video about road bumps and responsibility](/writing/politicians-road-bumps-and-maintenance-in-kosovo/): public space is where large promises become details we can actually see.

A respectful place does not have to be extravagant. It needs attention that continues after the photographs from the ceremony have been taken. That is the standard I would like to keep behind the joke.
