A man in a television studio asks which institution in Albania is functioning. He points towards the screen, returns to Parliament, then asks who is answering for what the police do.[1]

The video is labelled with Erald Kapri’s account and the Mundësia party logo. It cuts between his studio argument and tense street footage. The demand running through it is accountability: who can require an explanation, and where does that explanation actually happen?

My caption calls Albania a police state. That is a political judgment, not a classification proved by this edited clip. The principle I am defending is more precise: **the police belong to the citizens. Public power must be answerable to them.**

## What Kapri is arguing

Kapri says a peaceful protest was dispersed violently, challenges the police leadership’s willingness to answer questions and says Parliament is closed. These are claims made in the recording. The excerpt does not supply the full protest sequence, the operational record or a dated parliamentary timetable.

The street inserts show helmeted officers and a confrontation. They give the argument an immediate setting. They cannot, on their own, establish every person’s conduct, the grounds for an intervention or the legality of the whole operation.

That distinction matters because the strongest version of a demand for accountability can survive questions. It should welcome the complete recording and the relevant documents. Anger should move us towards those records, not make them unnecessary.

## What the police are supposed to be

The State Police Law describes a depoliticised institution. Its principles include impartiality, transparency and accountability, alongside protection of fundamental rights.[2]

That is a useful reference for my caption. Serving the public cannot mean requiring the public to agree with whoever governs. A citizen can criticise an administration and still expect protection. An officer can enforce a lawful restriction and still owe an explanation for the decision.

I do not want to turn every person in uniform into the same villain. I want the conduct, the order and the person responsible for a decision to be identifiable. Collective insults are easy. A precise answer is harder to evade.

## The important exchange comes near the end

The host suggests using Parliament to call the police leadership to account. Kapri responds that Parliament is closed and refers to his committee role. The argument moves from what happened in the street to whether institutional scrutiny is working.

The Constitution already provides routes for parliamentary questions and committee requests for explanations.[3]

The useful follow-up is therefore concrete: was a formal request made, what happened to it, and where is the response? This article does not establish that history. It shows why the answer belongs beside the television argument.

In [my piece about police pay and conscience](/writing/police-pay-conscience-public-service/), the question was what public service asks of a person. Here it is what the public can ask in return.

**An institution is not strong because nobody questions it. It is strong enough when it can answer.**
