I was reading another book about Elon Musk when I recorded this video on 18 June. I wanted to talk about role models: engineering, entrepreneurship and the work of making something useful.

The line I keep returning to is a question: at the end of the day, week or year, have I created more than I consumed? In the video I apply it to work, social media, friendship and family. It is an invitation to examine my contribution, not a scientific equation for a person’s worth.

## The trillionaire headline needs a date

The opening refers to Musk as the first trillionaire. **Forbes announced that assessment on 12 June 2026**, estimating his net worth at $1.1 trillion after SpaceX began public trading. That is Forbes’s dated estimate, not a permanent status or a bank balance.

Net worth depends on the value assigned to assets and liabilities. A company’s market value and its founder’s personal wealth are different quantities. If shares change price, a wealth estimate changes even if no cash has been paid into the person’s account.

The June headline explains the timing of my reaction. The practical lesson does not require us to follow someone’s ranking every day, accept every biographical claim about them, or agree with all their politics.

## Make the question small enough to answer

“Create value” can sound so large that it becomes another thing to admire from a distance. A smaller version is easier to use: what problem did I help solve this week, and for whom?

For an employee, it might be a process that makes a colleague’s job less frustrating. For a founder, it might be a customer problem resolved reliably. In a family, it can be care, attention or a task someone no longer has to carry alone. Some contributions are paid. Many are not.

## A simple time example, without pretending time equals value

Imagine reallocating **20 minutes a day for five days** from passive scrolling to a small project. That creates 100 minutes, or **1 hour 40 minutes**, of practice time. The arithmetic is certain; the outcome is not. You might finish a useful template, learn a technique or discover that your first approach fails.

A useful review asks what changed for the person using the result. A hundred minutes of activity is not automatically a hundred minutes of value. And consumption is not automatically waste: reading, resting and listening can be part of doing good work.

The aim is deliberate balance, not guilt about every moment you are not producing. I would not use the video’s calorie analogy as nutrition advice or turn it into a rule about eating less.

## What children can learn from work

The parenting point in the video is that children need to learn how things are made and what effort looks like, not only how money is spent. The useful version is age-appropriate participation: helping with a task, finishing a small project and seeing who benefits from it.

That does not require copying stories about an entrepreneur sleeping in a factory. Exhaustion is not a prerequisite for meaningful work. Nor does a wealthy person’s success prove that opportunity, support, teams or luck played no role.

For me, the question leads back to action: make one useful thing, show it to someone, listen, improve it. The [AI service-business example](/writing/start-a-service-business-with-ai-skills-and-real-costs/) takes that idea into a concrete offering with costs and responsibilities attached.
