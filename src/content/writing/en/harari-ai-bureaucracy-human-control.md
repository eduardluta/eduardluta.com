A cow can see the person buying it. It cannot follow the bank transfer that changes who owns it. Yuval Noah Harari uses that image to ask what happens when a system governing your life becomes incomprehensible to you.[1]

I shared the clip with a question: **which decision would you never leave entirely to AI?** It is a more useful starting point than treating the future as already settled.

The phrase “cows of the future” is deliberately uncomfortable. The issue it raises is concrete: being affected by decisions without a meaningful way to understand or challenge them.

## What Harari’s warning does—and does not—establish

In the excerpt, Harari contrasts humanity’s origins in nature with AI’s suitability for administrative systems. He predicts increasingly complex financial and legal arrangements that people may no longer understand. That is his forecast, not a demonstrated result or a reliable countdown.

The distinction matters. We do not need to accept every prediction in a dramatic interview to examine the question it poses. Nor does the existence of a powerful AI system establish that it is accurate, fair or appropriate for a particular decision.

## Understanding a decision is different from reading every line of code

Imagine a small business applying for a loan. This is an illustrative situation, not something I am claiming happened to me. An automated system produces a recommendation, and the application is rejected.

What would help the applicant? A list of every computation might be useless. A clear explanation that an outdated revenue figure affected the assessment would be useful—especially if the applicant could correct it and obtain another review.

The OECD’s transparency principle calls for useful information about AI involvement and outcomes, including information that helps people challenge them.[2] My practical question is: can the person do something with the explanation?

An explanation should identify a reason that can be examined. “The system gave you this result” simply describes what happened. It does not explain why the result should stand.

The answer also needs to reach the right person. An accessible appeal route is of little value if everyone receiving the appeal says they lack authority to change anything. That is a design problem worth addressing before the system is put into use.

## Responsibility cannot disappear into the software

The OECD’s accountability principle places responsibility on the organisations and people developing, deploying and operating AI, according to their roles. It also calls for traceability of relevant data, processes and decisions.[3]

Applied to our imaginary loan application, I would want the institution to be able to establish which information was used, who reviewed the recommendation and how a mistake would be corrected. These are editorial questions for the example, not a statement of the legal rights available in every country.

There is a business reason to care as well. If nobody can reconstruct why a decision happened, a company has difficulty distinguishing a bad input, an unsuitable model and a failure in its own procedure. Buying software does not answer those questions for it.

NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework provides another useful reference: it is a voluntary framework for considering risks to people, organisations and society throughout the development and use of AI.[4] A voluntary framework is not proof that a particular product is safe. It is a way to organise work that still has to be done.

## Use the capability without giving up judgment

I am interested in what AI makes possible. In [learning AI through a real project](/writing/how-to-start-learning-ai-with-a-real-project/), the useful step is to try something concrete and examine the result. That same habit belongs in larger decisions: define the task, check the output and retain the ability to correct the process.

The level of scrutiny should fit the consequence. A suggested title for a blog post can be discarded in seconds. A decision affecting someone’s access to money, work or a public service deserves a much more serious process. That comparison is an editorial judgment, not a risk score calculated from the clip.

Harari’s image stays with me because it makes dependence easy to picture. But the response does not have to be helplessness. We can ask who chooses the rules, who checks the result and who has the power to change it.

**A capable system should leave the people affected by it with somewhere to turn.**
