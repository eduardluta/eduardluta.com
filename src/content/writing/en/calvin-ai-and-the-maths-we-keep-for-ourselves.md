Calvin is sitting at a school desk, hand raised, making a proposal that is suspiciously convenient for Calvin: let the machines handle the maths, then go outside.[1]

I shared the panel because the joke feels fresh in a world where AI can produce an answer before we have finished reading the question. There is something attractive in it. If a tool takes a task off my hands, why would I insist on doing everything the old way?

But I keep coming back to a smaller question: **am I trying to finish this task, or learn how to do it?**

## The answer on the screen is only one result

For a finished task, a useful tool can save work. For learning, I also want something to remain with the person after the tool is gone.

In a 2023 Turkish-school trial, GPT Base raised practice scores but lowered later unaided scores; guarded GPT Tutor showed no significant unaided difference.[2]

That distinction is enough to make me pause before treating a completed worksheet as proof that I understand the topic.

## Use the tool and keep a test for yourself

My practical response would be simple: try the problem, ask for help with the part that blocks me, then close the answer and attempt another problem myself. This is my suggested check, not a routine shown in the cartoon or a tested intervention of my own.

I would want to explain why the answer makes sense, not only repeat the number. If I cannot do that yet, I know where the next piece of work is.

This connects with [learning AI through a real project](/writing/how-to-start-learning-ai-with-a-real-project/): make something concrete, inspect what happened, and stay involved in the thinking.

Calvin’s proposal still makes me smile. Tools should give us room to live. I just want to keep enough understanding to know what I am handing over.

**Let the machine help. Keep the part that lets you question it.**
