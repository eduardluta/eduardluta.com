I was working at the office when a ten-year-old came looking for help. He had taken a computer, made a little workspace at home and wanted to create gaming videos for YouTube. Something would not work. Could we come and help?

That was the moment behind this video about generational wealth.

The wealth I noticed was not the computer. It was that he had an idea, believed he could make something and knew adults he could ask. He could walk into our world and say: I am building this; help me with the part I cannot do yet.

## An inheritance you can see in a room

He had watched people working with laptops, meeting, discussing and making things. Now he was arranging a small version of that world for himself.

I cannot prove which moment inspired him. But I could see the result in front of me: curiosity had turned into an attempt. Helping with the setup gave that attempt somewhere to go.

This is how I am using “generational wealth” here. Financial assets matter, and this is not a claim that encouragement removes economic inequality. I am describing another thing families can pass on: familiarity with creating, asking questions and finding help.

## Experience is not a race against other children

In the video I get excited about what ten years of making things could mean by the time he is twenty. That is a possibility, not a forecast of his career or a reason to demand ten years of productivity from a child.

He can change interests. He can play. He can make something nobody watches. The adult's job is not to convert every hobby into a business plan. It is to make room for exploration and help keep it appropriate and safe.

A useful response begins with the child's question. Show the next step, let them try and stay available. Taking over the whole project may finish it faster, but it can remove the very discovery that made it interesting.

## The help can travel further

What moved me was imagining him later helping another child. A little knowledge becomes easier to pass on when asking for help has been treated as normal.

There is no guaranteed multiplier to put on that. There is a practical choice: when someone young brings you a real question, do you make them feel like an interruption, or like a person whose curiosity matters?

I wrote elsewhere about [giving advice a concrete starting point](/writing/asking-for-advice-start-with-your-situation/). This child had already done that. A computer. A project. A problem. An invitation to help.

Sometimes the next generation does not need a speech about its potential. It needs you to come upstairs and look at the computer.
