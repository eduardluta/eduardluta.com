Before my wife was my wife, we decided to do a workshop together.

That is the word I use in the video. I even make the distinction: not one person working while the other goes shopping. Both of us sitting down and taking the conversation seriously.

How was the relationship going? What did each of us want? What could our future look like?

One thing we discussed was becoming parents. There were other plans too. Some worked. Others did not. Looking back eight or nine years later, I describe that first workshop as one of the best things we did together.

It gave our hopes somewhere to meet.

## A shared future needs two voices

When I talk about creating something great, family is one of the first things I mean. A family is not something one person designs and hands to the other as an assignment.

The useful part of that first conversation was bringing ideas into the open. What does a good life mean to you? What do you want to change? What would you like to do in your career? Where do our wishes meet, and where do they need more conversation?

Agreement is valuable. So is discovering that you were assuming agreement that did not exist.

In the video I say we returned to this ritual roughly each year, sometimes more than once. That is our experience, not a proven schedule that every couple should follow. We also did not predict everything correctly. **A plan can be useful without becoming a promise that life will obey it.**

## An example one-hour conversation

Here is a practical agenda inspired by that story. These timings are an editorial suggestion, not a reconstruction of our original workshop:

| Time | Question |
| --- | --- |
| 10 minutes | What has been good between us lately? |
| 15 minutes | What does each of us need or hope for? |
| 15 minutes | What limits do time, money, care and work place on those hopes? |
| 15 minutes | Which one small shared step can we actually take? |
| 5 minutes | When will we return to this conversation? |

The total is **10 + 15 + 15 + 15 + 5 = 60 minutes**. The three central sections take 45 minutes, or **75% of the hour**. That is simply a time budget, not a formula for a successful relationship.

Both people need space inside each section. If one issue is too big for the hour, name it and arrange another conversation instead of forcing a decision because the timer ran out. Nobody owes an immediate yes to parenthood, a move or a career change.

The same structure can help you begin talking with a friend or business partner, as I suggest in the clip. But the relationship matters more than the worksheet. Listening cannot be delegated to an agenda.

I connect this with my [reflection on road-trip conversations and apologies](/writing/road-trips-conversations-and-apologies/): we need room to speak before everything hardens into a story about what the other person must be thinking.

We did not build a future by guessing each other’s minds. We started by sitting down together.
