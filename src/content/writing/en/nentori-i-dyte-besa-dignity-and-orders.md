An officer on horseback insists that Ismail Qemali come with him. He has an order behind him. The men in front of him have to decide what their word is worth.

I shared this scene because of that choice. My caption was about besa, dignity and the courage to face fear. Loving your country should reach as far as your conscience. Otherwise the flag becomes much easier to carry than the responsibility.

## The film behind the clip

The scene comes from *Nëntori i dytë* (*The Second November*), Viktor Gjika’s 1982 historical drama. Albania’s national film archive describes its story as the journey towards the declaration of independence in Vlora on 28 November 1912. [1, 4]

The confrontation is acted. It is a dramatic portrayal of the past, and the dialogue should be read as film dialogue. The clip I posted on 18 October 2026 is not footage of a contemporary protest or a recording of the historical conversation.

In the excerpt, an appeal to shared identity interrupts obedience. A soldier offers to stand with Ismail Qemali. The officer invokes punishment. By the end, the idea of independence has become larger than whether one man can be arrested.

There is another layer to the film’s survival. The Albanian Cinema Project and Colorlab restored it from original film materials, with a restoration premiere in Tirana on 3 November 2012. [2] That does not establish which print this social-media excerpt came from. It does remind us that a scene reaches another generation because somebody preserved the work behind it.

## What I mean by besa

The line I selected for the caption is about refusing to betray another Albanian. What matters to me is the obligation inside it: somebody must be able to rely on your word when keeping it becomes uncomfortable.

There are documented examples beyond cinema. Yad Vashem’s exhibition about Muslim Albanians who rescued Jews during the Holocaust explains besa through keeping a promise and being trusted with another person’s safety. [3] Those accounts concern different people and a different historical period from the film.

That also widens the question. Dignity cannot depend only on whether the person asking for protection belongs to our own group. The responsibility becomes meaningful when another person is vulnerable and our decision has a cost.

## The order and the person carrying it out

A uniform, a title or a powerful voice can make an instruction feel final. The scene holds that moment open. The person receiving the order is still there, with a judgment to make.

I return to a similar question when writing about [public decisions and accountability](/writing/tirana-kiosks-public-accountability/): who takes responsibility for what happens to the people affected?

The film cannot answer today’s political disputes for us. What it can do is leave us with an uncomfortable question about our own conduct. When pressure arrives, how much of what we say we believe is still standing?

That is why I shared it.
