I am outside with Marin, asking him what it means to love someone a lot. We are smiling. The caption says love can be very simple.

I do not want to make the moment more complicated than it is. A father, a child, a question. But I do want to stay with what it brings up for me: love has to become something the other person can experience.

For a child, that can be as ordinary as having a place beside you. Being invited into what you are doing. Asking a question and getting a real answer, rather than a distracted noise.

## Make some of the ordinary day shareable

There are parts of adult life a child cannot join. Work, private conversations, situations that are unsuitable or unsafe. Making room does not mean taking a child literally everywhere or abandoning boundaries.

It means noticing the parts that could be shared. A short walk. Preparing something together. Looking at an object that has caught their attention. The activity does not have to be impressive enough to post.

These are suggested examples, not a record of our day in the video. Their point is the same: the invitation becomes real through attention. Being in the same room and feeling included are not automatically the same experience.

## Give the small signal a response

Harvard's Center on the Developing Child describes “serve and return”: a child signals interest and an adult responds, creating a back-and-forth exchange that supports development. That gives language to an ordinary interaction without turning parenting into a performance. [Serve and return](https://developingchild.harvard.edu/key-concept/serve-and-return/).

If a child points at something, a possible response is to look, name what they noticed and leave space for what comes next. No elaborate lesson is required. The child is a participant, not just the audience for an adult's explanation.

The diagram is an everyday adaptation of that idea, not a checklist for judging a parent's love. There will be missed moments. An adult can be tired, occupied or simply need a boundary. A later return—“show me what you wanted to tell me”—can make the intention concrete again.

## Shared experiences give us something to talk about

Our [visit to the Swiss Museum of Transport](/writing/swiss-museum-transport-family-visit/) made me think about the quality of family conversation. A place full of things to discover gives you openings. At home, the opening might be much smaller.

What matters to me is not whether the setting looks special. It is whether I actually join the moment. Put the phone aside when possible. Let the question finish. Allow the child to tell the story in their own order.

And leave room for their answer to differ from yours. In the [Kosovo-or-Switzerland conversation](/writing/kosovo-switzerland-childs-idea-of-home/), the useful part is listening to a preference before explaining an adult's whole comparison.

## A simple thing still needs practice

It is easy to say that family comes first. The more revealing question is where family gets space in an ordinary day.

I do not need to turn every moment into a lesson, and neither does Marin. Sometimes we can just be outside, smiling, asking each other a question.

Love can be simple. I still have to make room for it.
