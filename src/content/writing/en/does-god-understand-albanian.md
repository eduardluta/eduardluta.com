In the video I list the things we credit God with creating: the universe, the earth, water, air. Then comes the joke. After all that, somehow Albanian becomes a problem when I want to speak to him.

The contradiction is the point. **Why would the language closest to my heart make the feeling less sincere?**

This is a personal spiritual question, not a ruling about how any religion requires a ritual to be performed.

## Understanding and ritual are different questions

A tradition can preserve a particular language for a formal prayer or liturgy. That does not by itself answer the larger question of whether God understands someone speaking another language. Mixing those two questions makes disagreement easier and understanding harder.

The distinction matters because somebody may value a ritual language deeply while also wanting to understand every word they say. Those wishes need not be enemies.

## Two passages worth reading in context

The Qur’an’s verse 30:22 places the diversity of human languages among the signs associated with creation. In the Christian account of Pentecost, Acts 2:6–11 describes listeners hearing in their own languages. [¹](#article-notes-heading)

These are different texts in different traditions. I am not presenting them as identical teachings or as a complete answer about the permitted form of worship. They do show that language and understanding have a place inside the source texts themselves.

For me, that makes the conversation more interesting than declaring one person’s mother tongue unworthy.

## Speak with conviction, listen with respect

I want room to ask the question without turning it into an insult against somebody else’s practice. That is the same principle behind my [appeal for religious tolerance](/writing/albanian-religious-tolerance-without-insults/).

If a word matters enough to repeat, its meaning matters enough to investigate. If a prayer comes from the heart, I want to understand what my mouth is saying.

My Albanian is not the obstacle in that conversation.
