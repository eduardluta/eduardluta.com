Your biggest fan is not necessarily the person who praises you most loudly. In this video, I mean someone who sees what you are trying to become and stays interested before the result exists.

That person might be a friend, a sibling, a parent or a partner. They understand the unfinished version. You do not have to explain the whole story every time something happens.

Losing them can change the meaning of the achievement you were working towards. The goal is still there. The person you imagined sharing it with is not.

## Losing a witness to your life

In the video, I describe this as losing a kind of mirror. Someone reflected your effort back to you, with knowledge of the history behind it. A stranger may congratulate you on a result. This person knew what it cost.

That is why the loss can reach beyond missing conversations. It can affect the imagined future: the call you expected to make, the celebration you pictured, the moment when you could finally say that it worked.

This is a reflection on that experience, not a claim that everyone feels loss in the same way. The NHS describes grief as a response to different kinds of loss, including the end of a relationship, and stresses that feelings vary. There is no required sequence to complete correctly.

## Death and distance are different situations

The video mentions both bereavement and relationships that break through disagreement or principle. They should not be treated as the same problem with the same solution.

When someone has died, a conversation cannot restore their presence. When someone is alive, contact might be possible—but it is not automatically wise, welcome or safe. Missing what a person once gave you does not settle whether a relationship should resume.

My suggestion in the video is about making peace where that is possible. Peace can mean acknowledging something valuable without recreating the relationship. It can mean an apology without a demand for forgiveness. It can also mean accepting a boundary and leaving the other person alone.

## What can remain

A useful question is: what did this person see in me that I still want to live up to? Their curiosity, honesty or belief in my work may remain meaningful even when the relationship changes.

That does not require turning pain into a lesson on schedule. Some days the practical task is much smaller: eat, rest, speak to someone, do the necessary thing. The NHS recommends small achievable steps and seeking support when coping is difficult.

I would rather notice these people while they are here. Tell them specifically what their support has meant. Ask about their unfinished work too. Being understood is valuable; returning that attention is part of friendship.

If you are thinking about reconciliation, the distinction in [love and boundaries](/writing/oscar-wilde-love-boundaries-courage/) matters: care and access to your life are separate decisions.
